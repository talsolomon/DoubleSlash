**In most design studios, the people with the most domain expertise get the least from AI. Juniors use it constantly, iterate fast, and build intuition quickly. Seniors are sporadic and skeptical, and when they do engage, the results are underwhelming. This is a problem worth taking seriously.**

---

There is a survey finding that stops most design leaders who read it. Nielsen Norman Group's 2025 research on AI tool adoption found that while overall adoption in design has grown, the pattern is inverted from what most leaders expected: younger, less experienced practitioners are integrating AI into daily work at substantially higher rates than senior practitioners.¹³

BCG's AI at Work research from the same period found a consistent pattern across knowledge-work industries: comfort with AI tools correlates inversely with years of experience.¹⁴ People earlier in their careers are not just more comfortable with AI — they are getting measurably more out of it, day to day, than the people who have spent the most time in the field.

This should be puzzling. It is also, on reflection, exactly what you would expect from the current state of AI tooling.

## Why do junior designers use AI more effectively than senior designers?

The honest answer is that AI currently rewards a specific type of engagement: high-frequency, low-stakes experimentation. Juniors iterate constantly because the cost of being wrong is low and the feedback loop is fast. They try something, it's mediocre, they adjust, they try again. They've iterated fifteen times before a senior practitioner has finished deciding whether to use AI for the task at all.

Seniors have developed high standards over years of work. They look at AI output with a calibrated eye — and often, the initial output doesn't clear their bar. The conclusion is frequently "this isn't good enough," which is often a correct assessment. The error is stopping there rather than iterating. The senior's judgment about quality is sound; the intuition for how to guide AI from a poor first draft to something genuinely useful hasn't been built, because it requires the kind of repetition they haven't done.

There is also something structural happening. Prompting AI well requires knowledge that isn't directly related to design expertise: knowing what to ask for, knowing how to constrain the output, knowing what context the AI needs to perform well. This is a new skill, and like most skills, it is built through repetition. The people doing it most often are building it fastest — which is currently the juniors.

## What does the AI expertise inversion actually cost a studio?

The cost is asymmetric in a way that matters.

A junior who uses AI frequently and gets reasonable results is producing more output, faster, with less supervision required. That is a straightforward operational gain.

A senior creative director who uses AI sporadically and gets poor results is a different problem. The senior's AI-unassisted judgment is still excellent — they have been doing this for fifteen years. But the delta between their output with AI and without it is smaller than it should be. Sometimes it is negative, if mediocre AI output anchors them to a direction that their own instincts would have skipped.

More importantly: the senior's domain expertise is precisely the input that should make AI most powerful. They know the client deeply. They know the category. They know what has worked before and what failed, and why. An AI working with that context, directed by a practitioner who knows how to use it, should produce substantially better output than an AI working with a junior who is still building domain knowledge. The potential for AI leverage is highest at the top of the experience curve.

But the habits aren't there. The intuition for prompting hasn't been built. The skepticism about initial output — legitimate for someone with calibrated judgment — becomes a barrier rather than a quality filter. The studio's most experienced thinkers are the ones getting the least from the tools.

## Is this a prompt engineering problem or a product failure?

The easy answer is that seniors need to build prompt engineering skills. The more honest answer is that requiring experienced practitioners to learn a new technical skill to unlock the value of a tool is a product failure.

The analogy holds: nobody expects a senior designer to write CSS to use Figma. The tool abstracts the technical layer and surfaces a professional-grade interface. The senior's expertise flows directly into the tool; the tool doesn't require them to first learn a new discipline.

AI tools for creative work haven't made this abstraction. The interface is still largely a blank text box. The designer with fifteen years of domain expertise stares at the same empty prompt field as a first-year graduate. Their expertise doesn't flow into the tool automatically — they have to translate it into prompting skill that they haven't had time to develop.

The tool that closes this gap will not make every prompt better. It will make the expertise *become* the prompt. The senior's knowledge of the project, the methodology, the client, the phase — that becomes the context that loads automatically, rather than something they have to figure out how to articulate in a text box. When AI inherits professional expertise rather than requiring practitioners to translate it, the experience curve works in the right direction again.

## Frequently asked questions

**Do senior designers use AI less than junior designers?**
Research suggests yes. Nielsen Norman Group's 2025 survey found lower AI adoption rates for core design tasks among more experienced practitioners. The pattern is consistent with BCG findings across knowledge-work industries. The primary drivers appear to be higher quality standards that early AI output frequently doesn't meet, less time for low-stakes experimentation, and prompting intuition that takes time and iteration to build.

**Why do junior designers get better results from AI than senior designers?**
Junior designers iterate at higher frequency, which builds prompting intuition faster. They also have lower initial quality thresholds — not because their standards are lower in the abstract, but because they're accustomed to working from rough starting points. Senior designers apply calibrated quality filters to initial output, often correctly judge it as insufficient, and don't always iterate to a result that clears their bar.

**Is this a problem that prompt engineering training can fix?**
Partially. Prompting intuition is learnable, and seniors who invest in building it do close the gap significantly. The deeper problem is structural: the tool should inherit from professional expertise, not require practitioners to translate expertise into a new skill. A senior designer with fifteen years of domain knowledge should produce better AI output than a junior because of that knowledge, not despite a gap in prompting experience.

**What would an AI tool that works better for senior practitioners actually look like?**
It would understand the project, the phase, the methodology, and the practitioner's role without requiring re-explanation. The quality filter the senior brings — the judgment about what's right for this client, this category, this moment — would apply after a good starting point has been generated, not instead of engaging with the tool at all. The interface would look less like a blank text box and more like a brief the AI has already read.
