**This is not a product description. It is a description of what work can feel like when the context layer is in place. No technical language. No feature lists. Just the experience.**

---

8:52 AM.

Maya opens her laptop. She has forty-five minutes before the first call. The project is a redesign of the onboarding flow for a B2B SaaS client — four weeks in, past the research phase, currently in shaping. Yesterday she and a developer reviewed the draft screens. Something wasn't right about the empty states. She closed the session without resolving it.

She opens the tool. Types `//`.

---

She doesn't type the client name. She doesn't paste the brief. She doesn't explain what phase she's in, or what was decided in last week's review, or what the developer said about the empty states yesterday.

The context is already there.

*Project: Veritas onboarding redesign. Phase: Solidify. Last session: Developer review, 14 May. Open: empty state copy and illustration direction — no consensus reached. Locked: three-step flow, progressive disclosure model, skip-for-now on SSO.*

The AI reads it before she types a word.

---

She types: *"Let's finish the empty states."*

Not: *"I'm working on a B2B SaaS onboarding flow redesign for a client, we're currently in the design phase, and yesterday we reviewed the screens with a developer and couldn't agree on the approach for empty states — can you help me think through options?"*

Just: *"Let's finish the empty states."*

The session starts where the last one ended.

---

The AI knows what "empty states" refers to. It knows the product. It knows the three-step flow and the progressive disclosure model already decided. It knows the constraint: no illustrated characters, a client preference noted in week two. It knows the developer's concern from yesterday — the copy felt too instructional, not enough like the rest of the voice.

It doesn't ask clarifying questions. It produces options.

---

Three directions in two minutes. Each one respects the decisions that are locked. Each one addresses the specific objection from yesterday. Each one is calibrated to the phase — developed enough to make a decision, not over-specified in ways that will need to be undone.

Maya reads them. The second one is close. She adjusts two words. The illustration question resolves itself: the second direction doesn't need one.

She screenshots it, drops it into the shared doc with a two-sentence note.

---

8:59 AM.

Seven minutes since she opened the tool.

The empty state question is answered. The developer gets a message with the decision and the rationale. The session is captured. The decision is logged. The next session — hers, the developer's, whoever else touches this project — will start knowing the empty state question is closed.

---

She didn't spend the first twenty minutes explaining the project. She didn't reconstruct the context from memory. She didn't open a Figma file and stare at it while trying to remember what she was working toward.

She pressed `//` and continued.

---

This is not a feature. It is not a workflow.

It is what design work has always been trying to be: you, the problem, and the thinking that moves between them. Not you, the problem, the re-briefing, the reconstruction, the management of context across sessions and tools and teammates — and then, eventually, if you still have time, the thinking.

The thinking first. The rest handled.

---

Nine articles have made the case: the cold start is a tax, the data structure gap is real, tool-level AI doesn't compound, memory in one tool isn't enough, handoffs fail because context isn't portable, standups are a workaround, accumulation beats acceleration, "knowing the project" has six layers, and the expertise inversion is a design problem not a user problem.

This is what it looks like when those problems are solved.

One keystroke. Five minutes. Something real.
