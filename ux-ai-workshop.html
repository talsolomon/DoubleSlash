The 4 levels — a progression, not a hierarchy
Each level includes everything below it. You don't graduate — you expand your range.

You describe a problem.

Passive

The ReAct loop
Goal — you define it in plain language
Reason — what should I do next?
Act — read a file, run a query, call a tool
Observe — what happened? what changed?
Repeat — until the goal is verified done
This is the frontier. Few designers are here. The productivity gap is enormous.
L4AGENTIC SYSTEMS — COORDINATED OPERATIONS
Multiple specialized agents
coordinating on complex work.
You review before anything ships.
This is not science fiction. Companies like Anthropic, Google, and a handful of startups are running production systems at this level today. Most design teams are 2–3 years from this being their daily reality.
The building blocks

Before MCP: copy from Figma → paste into Claude → copy the result → paste back somewhere else. Every tool is an island.
After MCP: the AI agent reads your Figma file directly. Checks your analytics. Updates your Linear ticket. No copy-paste. No context loss.

Figma MCP — the actual setup
MCP works.
Your file has to be ready.
Figma MCP reads your file — but it reads whatever's there. If tokens aren't defined as Variables, the agent sees #A0432A — not color/rust/600. If components aren't named, it sees Frame 47.
The protocol is only as smart as the file behind it. Most Figma files aren't agent-ready. That's the real setup task.
None of this is hard. But it is intentional work that must happen before the agent can help.
What needs to exist first
1 · Figma Variables (not just Styles)
Colors, spacing, typography defined as Variables — so the agent reads semantic tokens, not raw hex values.
2 · Named components with clear hierarchy
Button/Primary/Large, not Frame 47. The agent uses names as design-language — garbage in, garbage out.
3 · Auto-layout applied everywhere
Without auto-layout, spatial relationships are just numbers. The agent can't infer intent from absolute positioning.
4 · Code Connect configured
Maps Figma components → real code components. Without it, the agent invents markup instead of using what you've built.
5 · MCP server installed + API key
The technical setup: Figma API token, MCP server config in Claude Desktop (or equivalent). Takes 15 minutes — once.
Human in the loop — the 95/5 principle
95% agent.
5% human.
The system does the execution. A human reviews before anything goes live. This is not a limitation — it's the design principle that makes agentic systems trustworthy.
Without the 5%: you ship fast and wrong. The 5% is where all your UX judgment lives.

Act 04 · 20 minutes
A Working
Framework.
Four rules that determine how much control you keep over what AI does.
The skill shift
Before AI
Designer as
executor.
You produce the artifact. Interview guide. Wireframe. Copy. Your value = how much you can make.
→
After AI
Designer as
director.
You judge the artifact. AI generates volume. Your value = quality of your direction, context, and critique.
Your UX training doesn't become obsolete — it becomes the thing AI can't replace. Someone has to know what good looks like, recognize when it's missing, and refuse to ship the average. That's you.
Karpathy's 4 rules — applied to design
1
Think before acting
State your assumptions before you prompt. AI fills in the gaps you leave open — silently, without telling you. Every unstated assumption becomes a revision cycle.
2
Simplicity first
Give AI one specific job, then forbid everything else. AI defaults to comprehensive — it hears "improve" as "redesign." One scoped change is testable. Six unrequested changes are a sprint you can't learn from.
3
Surgical changes
Name exactly what to change — and what to leave alone. AI will improve whatever it notices: adjacent components, related patterns, things that "clashed." Tell it explicitly what is out of scope.
4
Goal-driven execution
Write what success looks like before you prompt. "Make it better" gives AI permission to do anything — and gives you no way to evaluate what it produced. A concrete criterion turns output into a decision.
Rule 1 — Think before acting
AI fills in what you leave open — silently. Name your assumptions before you prompt.
Without this rule
Brief: "Redesign the checkout flow." AI picks: web, logged-in, 3-step. Doesn't ask. Doesn't tell you. → 5 screens presented to client. → "This is for mobile. We have guest checkout. Did you even read the brief?" Back to zero.
AI filled gaps you left open. You didn't notice until the presentation. Every silent assumption is a revision waiting to happen.
Applied — your prompt
Before I generate anything, here's what I'm assuming: 1. Web, not mobile 2. Logged-in users only — no guest mode 3. Single payment method per session 4. Three-step flow (cart → details → confirm) Two things I am NOT designing: guest checkout, mobile breakpoints. If either matters, stop me here.
Two minutes of alignment stops a day of rework. You named what you're deciding — and what you're not.
Rule 2 — Simplicity first
AI defaults to comprehensive. Give it one job, not an open invitation.
Vague prompt — what AI hears
Your prompt: "Improve our sign-up form." AI heard: improve everything. → New field order → 3 new form states → Animated inline validation → Progressive disclosure pattern → Full component library update → Suggested A/B test plan You asked for one fix. You got a sprint of unrequested work you can't test and can't reverse.
You can't learn from six changes at once. You can't isolate what worked. You've lost control of your own design process.
Scoped prompt — what to write instead
One change only: CTA label "Continue" → "Create my account" Do not touch the layout. Do not suggest other improvements. Do not add variants. That is the entire request.
One change is testable — you'll know if it worked. If it didn't, you'll know exactly why. That's how you build real signal.
Rule 3 — Surgical changes
AI will improve what it notices. Name exactly what to change — and what to leave alone.
Without this rule
Task: Fix the error state on the email field. → Redesigns the input style → Adjusts spacing across the form → Changes the button colour "since it clashed" → Renames the component → "Also noticed the placeholder text was inconsistent..." The original fix is now buried in changes nobody asked for.
"While I was here" is how regressions happen. You can't review what you didn't ask for.
Applied — your prompt
Fix the error state on the email field. I expect exactly two changes: — Error message text — Error icon Everything else stays identical. Layout, spacing, button, component name: do not touch. If you noticed something else that looked off — tell me. Don't touch it.
A clean diff is reviewable. A sprawling diff hides what actually changed — and who's responsible for it.
Rule 4 — Goal-driven execution
"Make it better" gives AI permission to do anything. Define what better looks like before you prompt.
Without this rule
Prompt: "Make this flow better." AI makes it different. Not better. Different. You get 3 variants. You can't choose between them — you have no criteria. You pick the one that looks good in the presentation. It ships. You can't measure if it worked. You never defined what "working" meant.
Vague goals produce confident-looking output you cannot evaluate. You'll react to how it looks, not what it does.
Applied — your prompt
Success = first-time mobile user completes this flow in under 90s. Generate 2 variants optimised for that. Nothing else. For each variant, tell me: — What you changed and why — What you traded off to get there — What you'd test first Now I can choose between them. And I can measure whether the winner actually worked.
Criteria before content — every time. The criterion is what turns AI output into a design decision.
Three questions that tell you when AI will help — and when it will only look like it's helping
01
Do I know what good looks like?
AI gives you the statistically average answer. If you cannot recognize a great answer when you see one, you will ship the average.
02
What is the cost of being wrong?
First-draft copy? Low cost. An onboarding flow for 100,000 users? Earn the answer with research, not prompts.
03
Will I validate with real users?
AI output without validation is a confident guess. Validation is what turns a guess into a design decision.
All three yes → use AI freely. Any no → do the human work first.

AI has no user. No context. No constraints. It produces questions that could apply to any product, any person, anywhere.

The 80/20
80%
AI gets you the first 80% in 20% of the time.
The last 20% is what makes a product feel designed, not generated. It's the judgment call, the emotional truth, the thing that doesn't come from a prompt. That's where you win.
4 ways AI-assisted design breaks down — even when you're doing it right
Phase drift — "the AI is always ideating"
You ask Claude for help before wireframing. Five prompts later, you have six wireframe variants and no research plan. AI has no concept of what stage it's in and no incentive to stay in one.
Intensity mismatch — "the AI over-produces"
A quick tooltip copy fix becomes a 6-page research doc with personas and journey maps because you asked for "a full UX analysis." AI's default is comprehensive. The right default is proportionate.
Context amnesia — "I can't pick up yesterday's work"
You explored a feature with Claude yesterday. Today you open a new chat and re-explain everything — the user, the constraints, the options you already ruled out. You usually just start over.
Trust opacity — "I don't know what the AI decided"
You asked for "a quick polish." It rewrote three microcopy strings, proposed a new IA, and added a feature flag. You caught two of those. Did you catch all three? You're not sure.
These aren't edge cases — every designer using AI hits all four, repeatedly. They're not failures of the tool. They're failures of the process. That's what a methodology is for.
